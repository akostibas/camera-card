Three prototypes, nine blind test runs

The prototype from the independent designer (C) won every comparison. Our current card's design (A) was the only one testers failed with.

C: 18 of 18 answers right. Fastest on every task that means looking back in time. All three testers ranked it first.

B: 17 of 18. Close behind on accuracy. The one miss came from a loading bug, fixed after the first tester.

A: 16 of 18. As good as the others for "what's happening now", and the hardest for anything in the past. One tester couldn't find the doorbell ring or the rats.

Where people got stuck

  • Every design: telling one person apart from the rest. All three needed clip after clip opened for the doorbell ring and the person leaving, because rows only say "Person". This was the slowest task everywhere.
  • A: small targets and hidden clips. Count bubbles hid what was inside them, a tap played an arbitrary clip in a group, there was no filter by kind, the colours had no legend, and the Live buttons covered the newest clips.
  • B: weak playback. Testers never found the scrubber (it only shows after tapping the video). A recording that was still going sorted below newer ones. Empty days each took a row.
  • C: things moving under your finger. When a clip ends the next one plays and the list scrolls along with it, so taps landed on the wrong row. Once, the clip just found ran on before it could be saved.
  • B and C: the camera chips run off the right edge. Doorbell, Crawl space and Gate & Shed needed a sideways scroll first.

What to build on

The testers' suggestions after reading the intent, merged with the prior-art survey of 12 apps. Each item says where the evidence came from.

  1. A still picture on every row, taken from the moment of detection. This is the realistic replacement for the text descriptions every tester asked for. It also covers "you want to see a face". All three testers; Apple Home, UniFi, Frigate. Depends on whether Home Assistant can serve a frame per recording.
  2. Collapse empty stretches and say where history ends. C's "Nothing matching from Oct 17 to Oct 31" and "Recordings are kept for 45 days" are why the rats task took seconds. All three testers; Arlo, Blue Iris and Wyze use calendars with activity dots.
  3. Now first. Whatever is still going on goes at the top and is labelled live. Tapping it goes live, and the start is one tap away. Keep the live grid. Testers 1 and 2; UniFi, Google Home, Scrypted.
  4. Playback that gets to the point. A visible scrubber, speed control, and skips sized to the recording. Ideally, start near the detection. All three testers; Google Home, DS cam.
  5. Keep the screen still. No auto-advance that moves the list. Saving names what it saved and uses the phone's own share sheet. Testers 2 and 3; Apple Home, UniFi.

C is the strongest base for all five. B's labelled Save button and A's per-camera Live buttons are each worth keeping.

Gaps the testers found in the intent

  • It never says what the card can actually know: only camera, time and a person, vehicle or animal tag, plus perhaps a picture. That would stop designers counting on descriptions.
  • It doesn't say whether a doorbell press reaches Home Assistant. If one does, it's the single most useful signal for use case 3.
  • Nothing asks for a sign that something is still going on, or for knowing when you've searched everything.
  • The floors cover loading speed but not the effort of finding one event, which is where the designs differed most.
  • It doesn't say whether searching inside long continuous recordings is in scope.
  • It doesn't say whether "video like the phone's" (autoplay) or "calmer" wins when they conflict.

How it was run

  • All three ran on the same mock data: 6 cameras over 45 days, each use case's answer planted among look-alike decoys. There are three answer sets, rotated so no tester saw the same answer twice, and the order of the prototypes rotated too.
  • The designer of C saw only the intent doc and the mock data. Testers saw only the screen.
  • After tester 1, three bugs that weren't design choices were fixed: the calendars (Chrome's own date picker doesn't show up in the testers' screenshots), B's loading of older days, and A and B showing the wrong date while scrubbing.
  • The limits: testers used a mouse on a phone-sized frame, not a real phone. Captions give away a recording's content faster than real video would. Pinch and the phone's own controls weren't tested.
  • Wall-clock time: 1 hour 17 minutes, from "proceed" to this page. That's 14 minutes to build A, B and the mock data while C was designed in parallel, 61 minutes for the 9 test runs and 3 intent reviews (the research ran alongside), and a few minutes to write this up.
